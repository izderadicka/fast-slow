Intuitive answers

From Thinking, Fast and Slow, chapter 3 – two puzzles that evoke an answer that is intuitive, appealing, and wrong.

1. Bat and ball

Do not try to solve the puzzle – listen to your intuition. You have seconds to type the first answer that comes to mind.

A bat and ball cost $1.10.

The bat costs one dollar more than the ball.

How much does the ball cost?

A number came to your mind. The number, of course, is 10: 10¢. The distinctive mark of this easy puzzle is that it evokes an answer that is intuitive, appealing, and wrong.

Do the math: if the ball costs 10¢, the bat costs $1.10 and the total is $1.20, not $1.10. The correct answer is 5¢ – the bat then costs $1.05, one dollar more, and together they cost $1.10.

The quick answer comes from System 1. Checking it takes only a moment of System 2, yet most people – including many students at top universities – do not make that small effort.

2. Roses

Here is a logical argument – two premises and a conclusion. Decide as quickly as you can whether it is logically valid: does the conclusion follow from the premises? You have seconds.

All roses are flowers.

Some flowers fade quickly.

Therefore some roses fade quickly.

A large majority of college students endorse this syllogism as valid. In fact the argument is flawed, because it is possible that there are no roses among the flowers that fade quickly.

The conclusion may well be true – some roses probably do fade quickly – but it does not follow from the premises. Just as in the bat-and-ball problem, a plausible answer comes to mind immediately, and checking it would take effort.
